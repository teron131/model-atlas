# Model Atlas Documentation Style

- Keep only UI documentation and its assets in `docs/`, except this file. No temporary docs, plans, reports, or implementation notes; put findings in the conversation.
- These rules cover prose, captions, alt text, and illustration text.
- Write directly and neutrally, focusing on the method rather than its authors. Introduce symbols in the surrounding explanation.
- Keep each paragraph on one source line; separate paragraphs with blank lines. No manual wrapping or sentence-per-line formatting.
- Explain a calculation's concrete effect and its important conditions before the details. Introduce its purpose, assumptions, and equations in the order that makes the explanation easiest to follow; do not force every mechanism into the same paragraph template.
- Explain why a mechanism is useful, distinguishing mathematical consequences, scoring-policy choices, assumptions, and validated findings. When intent is not established, describe the consequence rather than inventing a motive. Prefer the general rule to specific numeric examples: use a number only when it shows a policy's scale or makes an unfamiliar operation concrete, never to illustrate how a score should be read, and omit arithmetic that adds no understanding.
- Reuse established names for domain concepts without making ordinary prose rigid. A new technical label should identify a useful distinction, explained in plain language. Qualify terms when context changes their meaning, such as effort variants versus product variants; do not merge distinct concepts merely to use fewer words.
- Prefer concrete descriptions over abstract shorthand: “points above 50,” not “margin”; “supported benchmark weight,” not “evidence mass”; “another effort of the same model,” not “donor.” Use standard mathematical terms when needed, but explain their meaning and purpose rather than replacing them with another unfamiliar label.
- Keep notation compact: meaningful letters and short qualifiers, such as $w$ for weight, $c$ for evidence support, and $S^{\text{res}}$ for a resource score. Define each symbol at first use and keep its meaning consistent within related calculations. Do not turn variables into full words or code identifiers such as `EvidenceSupport` or `TaskShare`.
- Reuse notation within related calculations. The current defaults are $m,j$ for variants, $g$ for a base model, $b$ for a benchmark, $k$ for an index, $d$ for a dimension, and $i$ for an item; $\omega$ for capability base weight, $w$ for other weights, $x$ for raw results, $z$ for normalized results, $S$ for scores, and $R$ for resource amounts. Other recurring symbols are $\rho$ for ratios, $y$ for log amounts, $\mu$ for expected amounts, $\Delta$ for differences or residuals, $s$ for spread, $e$ for validation error, $f$ for evidence factors, $c$ for evidence support, $p$ for peer support, $n$ for counts, $r$ for weight fractions or ranks, $M$ for multipliers, $C$ for shared coverage, $L,U$ for bounds, and $\alpha,\beta$ for intercepts and slopes. Prefer subscripts for indices, with the dimension last, and superscripts for roles. Hats mark estimates, bars means, tildes medians, and primes adjusted values; bold denotes matrices and vectors. These are defaults, not a ban on conventional alternatives: define each symbol locally and choose accent widths and qualifiers for legibility. Use $\ln$ for natural logs and explicit sums where a weighted mean's inputs and weights matter.
- Explain an existing calculation where it belongs and link back when needed. Do not repeat the scoring formula under a new name in an imputation section. Combine equations when intermediate steps add no understanding; retain forms such as “end minus start” when they reveal why a formula works. Keep derivations to the detail needed for the requested explanation.
- A benchmark is an evaluation with its own methodology and scoring rules. A task is one execution of work under that benchmark’s methodology, not another benchmark or an aggregate score. Use “benchmark” for portfolio entries, scores, counts, shared coverage, and comparisons; reserve “task” for executions and resource measurements per task, preserving the source’s reported unit. Apply this distinction to prose, notation, headings, tables, and illustrations.
- Keep domain names consistent across pages: “base weight” for the weight before evidence adjustments (importance × allocation for capability scores), “evidence support” for the displayed share backed by evidence, “score retention” and “retention multiplier” for the capability discount, “shared coverage multiplier” for the :score[Speed] and :score[Value] discount, “peer support” for the strength of a comparison with other models, and “reference weight” for model-balanced weights. In scoring, a “variant” is a model at one reasoning effort; qualify product variants in identity discussions.
- Use “weighted mean,” “imputation” for missing values, “crosswalk” for source conversion, and “evidence factor” for the fraction of weight counted as evidence consistently. Before finishing, check that every introduced term is necessary, has one clear meaning, and does not duplicate a concept explained elsewhere.
- Keep paragraphs focused on one explanatory job. Separate a mechanism's effect, its mathematical definition, and detailed exceptions when combining them would make a paragraph hard to follow. Remove repetition without making pages depend on distant definitions for their basic meaning. Respect explicit editorial decisions and later requests to reconsider them.
- Use descriptive headings without unnecessary numbering. Include function names only when useful, with clear inputs and outputs.
- Mark intentional Model Atlas score references with `:score[Intelligence]`, `:score[Agentic]`, `:score[Speed]`, or `:score[Value]`; only these explicit markers receive icons. Markers work inside headings, links, emphasis, tables, and image captions. Leave general capabilities, benchmark loadings, external or historical index names, generic table headers, and selection-role names such as Pareto Value plain. Do not put markers in equations, code, link destinations, or SVG text.
- Prefer Markdown whenever it can express the content clearly: headings, explanations, definitions, worked arithmetic, tables, and process sequences belong in Markdown. Reuse existing Markdown flow rendering for sequences; use SVG only where spatial relationships, plotted curves, or visual mechanics add understanding.
- Keep visible SVG text minimal: only labels, axes, units, values, and legends needed to read the visual. Put explanatory titles, subtitles, sentences, and footnotes in the surrounding Markdown. Keep captions short; do not bury a process or explanation in a long caption.
- Moving content out of an SVG means preserving its meaning and structure in Markdown, not deleting it. Preserve the steps and relationships in a scope sequence, and remove only genuinely redundant wording.
- SVG canvases must be transparent, with no background panels or decorative frames. Keep meaningful data marks and shading only when they explain the calculation.
- Place illustrations beside their explanations or immediately after their equations. Study relevant established examples before creating or substantially redesigning a figure, and adapt useful visual conventions to the existing documentation style.
- Use consistent examples across prose and figures; verify values, weights, ranks, boundaries, and captions. Label illustrative values and policy thresholds. Typical prediction errors are not confidence intervals or guaranteed bounds.
- Match the current implementation and preserve calculation rules and units. Distinguish observations from estimates, evidence factors from direct evidence, and policy choices from validated findings.
- Review the entire affected section and its illustrations together before finishing; stay within the requested scope. Verify that moving text preserved the explanation, then inspect rendered figures for readability, clipping, spacing, and theme contrast. An all-SVG review covers every documentation SVG, not just the example the user identifies.
- `test/documentation-consistency.ts` checks Markdown formula rendering, local heading links, and figure registration and dimensions. Run it after editing docs. Terminology, notation choices, rationale, and readability require contextual review rather than blanket word or symbol bans; a passing test does not establish mathematical correctness or browser accessibility.
